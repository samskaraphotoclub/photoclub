Talking about photographs

How to give useful feedback at a club evening, and how to take it.

Seeing other people’s pictures, and hearing what they see in yours, is one of the best reasons to join a club. Good feedback helps a photographer see their own work with fresh eyes. Careless feedback just makes people stop sharing. A few habits make the difference.

Look first, talk later

Give a photo some time before you say anything. Notice where your eye goes first and where it travels after that. Ask yourself what the photographer wanted to show, and what you feel when you look at it. Your first reaction is useful information, as long as you treat it as your reaction and not as a fact about the picture.

Describe before you suggest

Start by describing what you see and what it does for you. Only then suggest changes. “The light catches the rim of the cup and my eye goes straight there” is far more useful than “nice light”. Name specific things, like the height of the horizon or the colour of a shadow, so the photographer has something to work with.

Phrase suggestions as possibilities. “I wonder how it would look with a little more space above the head” leaves room for what the photographer intended. They may have had a reason you can’t see.

Use a shared vocabulary

The chapters on composition and light give the club a common language. Saying a picture has strong side light, or that a leading line runs out of the frame, is clearer than saying it doesn’t quite work. It also points the photographer to the right part of this handbook.

Receiving feedback

Listen first and save your explanations for afterwards. If people misread a picture, that tells you something about what the picture says on its own, because you won’t be standing next to it when others see it. Thank people and take notes. Decide later what to act on. You don’t have to agree with every comment, and some of the best pictures ignore the advice of the room.

Running a critique evening

Small groups of four to six work better than one big circle, because everyone gets a chance to speak. Keep each picture to a few minutes. Let the photographer say one sentence about it first, or nothing at all if they want an unbiased reaction. Changing who speaks first each round stops the most confident voices from setting the tone every time.

Key points

Feedback that helps

  • Be specific. Point at the part of the picture you mean.
  • Start with what works. Strengths are as useful to know as weaknesses.
  • Suggest, don’t prescribe. Offer ideas the photographer can try out.
  • Talk about the picture. Comment on the work, never on the person.

Exercises

Exercises R1–R4. Tick each one off when you’ve tried it. Your ticks stay on this phone or computer.

  1. R1

    A silent minute

    Look at a photo for a full minute before you speak. Write down where your eye goes first and where it goes next.

  2. R2

    Describe, then suggest

    Give feedback on a few pictures using the order in chapter 13. Ask the photographers which comments helped them most.

  3. R3

    Your own picture

    Pick one of your photos and write a short critique of it as if someone else had taken it.

  4. R4

    Ask the room

    At the next club evening, ask one specific question about your picture instead of asking what people think of it.
